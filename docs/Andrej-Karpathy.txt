Andrej Karpathy's post on "context engineering over prompt engineering"

People associate prompts with short task descriptions you'd give an LLM in your day-to-day use. When in every industrial-strength LLM app, context engineering is the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step. Science because doing this right involves task descriptions and explanations, few shot examples, RAG, related (possibly multimodal) data, tools, state and history, compacting... Too little or of the wrong form and the LLM doesn't have the right context for optimal performance. Too much or too irrelevant and the LLM costs might go up and performance might come down. Doing this well is highly non-trivial. And art because of the guiding intuition around LLM psychology of people spirits.

On top of context engineering itself, an LLM app has to:
- break up problems just right into control flows
- pack the context windows just right
- dispatch calls to LLMs of the right kind and capability
- handle generation-verification UIUX flows
- a lot more - guardrails, security, evals, parallelism, prefetching, ...

So context engineering is just one small piece of an emerging thick layer of non-trivial software that coordinates individual LLM calls (and a lot more) into full LLM apps. The term "ChatGPT wrapper" is tired and really, really wrong.

---

### Interpretive Expansions of Context Engineering

#### Key Mental Model: The LLM as a Computer

To engineer context effectively, think of the AI system using this hardware analogy:

1. **The LLM is the CPU:** It is the reasoning engine. It performs the "thinking," logic, and pattern matching. However, it has no long-term memory of the current task other than what is in front of it.
2. **The Context Window is the RAM:** This is the working memory. Just as a computer can only process data currently loaded into RAM, an LLM can only reason over information currently inside its context window.
3. **The Developer/System is the OS Scheduler:** Your job is to manage the "RAM" efficiently—loading the right "nuggets" of data, clearing out old junk, and ensuring the "CPU" has everything it needs for the next instruction.

### The Science of Context Engineering

Doing this "right" involves a precise orchestration of data:
* **Few-Shot Examples:** Providing 2-3 examples of the input-output pattern. This is often more effective than long explanations.
* **RAG (Retrieval Augmented Generation):** Dynamically pulling in relevant external data from a database/knowledge base.
* **Multimodal Data:** Integrating images, audio, or video into the flow.
* **Tools & State:** Including the current "world state" (e.g., API schemas, date/time, user preferences).
* **Compacting & Pruning:** Summarizing history or removing irrelevant context to prevent "context rot" (where the model loses focus due to too much noise).

### The Art of Context Engineering

This involves "LLM Psychology"—understanding the "vibe" and nuances of how different models react.
* **Ordering:** Realizing that models often pay more attention to the beginning and end of a context (the "Lost in the Middle" phenomenon).
* **Formatting:** Using clear delimiters (like XML tags `<role>`, `<rules>`) to help the model distinguish between instructions, examples, and user data.
* **Instructional Spirit:** Crafting the persona and constraints so the model understands the *intent* and *boundaries* of the task, not just the steps.

### Industrial-Strength LLM Apps

Beyond the individual call, a robust app must manage:
* **Control Flows:** Breaking one complex problem into a sequence of smaller, verifiable LLM calls.
* **Dispatching:** Selecting the right model for the right task (e.g., a fast/cheap model for classification, a powerful/reasoning model for extraction).
* **Verification Loops:** Using the LLM to verify its own (or another model's) output.
* **Guardrails & Evals:** Ensuring safety, consistency, and accuracy through rigorous testing and systematic checks.
